# Storytelling 3P: problem, pursuit, payoff

A working method for scripts that keep people watching. It comes from Jun Yuh's masterclass (YouTube, Aug 2026). Fuller notes with timestamps and every example he shows are in `references/masterclass-notes.md`; read them when a script feels flat and you cannot say why.

## Why most scripts feel like AI slop

Jun defines storytelling as taking something that really happened and telling it so people feel it and see themselves in you. Any idea can be told at three depths:

- **Safe** is generic, the kind of thing anyone could get by asking an AI. Jun says it never wins.
- **Real** is relatable. It names the feeling under the moment.
- **Raw** is engaging. It says the part people do not say out loud, and that is where the best creators live.

Scripts that list features, give tips anyone could give, or narrate the steps the creator took stop at safe. Nothing in them makes anyone feel anything, so nothing makes them keep watching. Jun calls creators who only pass on information anti-human.

## Step 1: find the moment and dig to the raw part

Start from one concrete moment, however small. Jun's examples are driving a kid to school or recording a first video. Stories come from four areas of a life (his L.I.F.E. wheel): Love (relationships, hobbies), Identity (origin, culture, age, faith), Fitness (mind, body, spirit, routines) and Earnings (career, clients, skills).

Ask why three times and write each answer down:

1. **The obvious reason.** I drove my kid to school because they need to be on time.
2. **The feeling underneath.** I love hearing about their day.
3. **The part nobody says out loud.** I am finally choosing them over work.

Build the piece on answer 3. When you say the quiet part out loud, viewers feel known, and that is how one post builds trust with many people.

## Step 2: give it an arc

Every story Jun shows, from The Lion King to a 15 second reel, has the same three acts.

- **Problem.** A specific struggle the viewer shares. Struggle is what people have in common; opening with a flex makes retention drop because nothing in it is human.
- **Pursuit.** The method, your own way of solving the problem. This is where the viewer learns something and where your value and later your product live. It is the part people skip most often.
- **Payoff.** The outcome, insight or lesson. It does not have to be a win. Not having it figured out yet is a payoff as long as it is honest and specific.

Problem and pursuit should take most of the time and the payoff the least. In Jun's best format the line budget is problem 7, pursuit 7, payoff 5, reflection and lesson 2 plus 2, call to action 1. The exact numbers matter less than the ratio and the order: the viewer resonates, then learns, then sees why they stayed.

The order can shift by format, and a showcase can open by flashing the finished result as a hook. That flash is a teaser. The story after it still needs all three acts.

### The three mistakes on Jun's slide

- **Problem too vague.** I struggled gives nobody anything to connect to. Say what exactly went wrong: a number, a time, a place.
- **Payoff without specificity.** Everything worked out is forgettable. I stopped pretending I wasn't scared is not.
- **Skipping the pursuit.** Going straight from problem to payoff reads as a flex. People need the journey.

The pursuit is a method the viewer could use, told as a journey. It is not a log of your revisions, tools, versions or hours spent. A line that only describes your process and teaches the viewer nothing should go.

## Step 3: pack it into a format

The topic is what you say; the format is how you say it. A format is repeatable because the three acts always sit in the same places.

| Format | Problem | Pursuit | Payoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| Silent film (text over B-roll, music, no voice) | first lines, visuals carry the emotion | middle lines, numbered steps or wins, must be readable | starts exactly when the music drops |
| Split-screen reel | top half of every frame | the cuts in between and the caption | bottom half of every frame |
| Advice I needed at X age (carousel) | first slide | the advice slides | last slide, then CTA |
| Old me vs new me | the start | the caption | the end |
| Problem statement | first line or first photo | the middle | the end |
| Split-screen carousel | top of frames 1 to 5 | the caption | bottom of frames 1 to 5, frame 6 overall payoff, frame 7 CTA |
| Talking head or voiceover | first sentence | the steps, each carrying a piece of your own experience | the result the viewer gets |

## Step 4: make the screen tell the story

From a frame by frame look at Jun's silent film reel:

- **Picture book effect.** The words carry the story; the image only amplifies the emotion, like in a children's book. The footage does not have to show the literal moment, so choose shots for the feeling of the line.
- **One short line per shot.** Small, centred, white, high contrast, never a block of text. Three dots at the end of a line pull the thought into the next shot.
- **Anchors.** A year or a time stamp makes it concrete: 2013:, 4:50 am morning walk, 6:45 am drive to work.
- **Numbered wins in the pursuit.** 1. Mental win, 2. Physical win. Numbers make progress visible.
- **Readability is not optional in the pursuit.** Right position, enough contrast.

## Step 5: let the sound carry the arc

Map the whole piece onto one track. The quiet build sits under the problem and the pursuit, and the payoff lands on the drop. In Jun's example the track stays low under the problem, falls almost silent right before the turn, and the payoff arrives as it swells. Cut so the turn of the story and the turn of the music happen on the same frame. With a voiceover, the same applies to the music bed under the voice.

## Before you ship

If any answer is no, the piece is not finished.

1. Can I name the one moment this piece is about?
2. Did I write the three whys, and is the script built on the third?
3. Would a viewer think that is me within two seconds of the problem?
4. Does the pursuit teach a method, with no lines about my own revisions?
5. Is the payoff specific, and is it the shortest part?
6. Do problem and pursuit take most of the running time?
7. Does every on-screen line fit one shot and read on a phone?
8. Does the music turn when the story turns?
9. Read it aloud. Does any sentence sound like something an AI would say about anything? Replace it with a real detail.

## Brand and faceless accounts

Jun teaches personal brands built on one person's life. On a brand or faceless account the method still works, but the human stake has to be found somewhere else: in a real test, a real client situation, or the viewer's own frustration, told from a real point of view. Never invent a life story to fill the problem slot; the raw part only works when it is true.
